## Background and Context

This investigation concerns three interrelated elements:

1. **Francisco L. Terminel** — a mining operator and landowner in the municipality of San Javier, Sonora, Mexico (founded in 1706 as Real de Minas de San Javier), who also served as a Mexican federal senator and was stripped of immunity (desafuero) on 14 December 1935, as confirmed by institutional sources at the Universidad Nacional Autónoma de México (UNAM-IIS).

2. **Aviana (also "Avina") Sagasta** — an indigenous woman (with probable Névome Pima Bajo affiliation based on geographic analysis of the San Javier region in central Sonora) who held legal title to numerous land parcels and mining-related assets in the San Javier area during the period approximately 1900–1914, while Francisco Terminel is believed to have exercised control through a power-of-attorney arrangement.

3. **Family oral history** — transmitted through four generations to present-day descendant Gabriel T. Arce Jr., which states that Aviana Sagasta personally visited a Wells Fargo office in Sonora or at the U.S.–Mexico border to inquire about funds or accounts associated with her name, and was denied access to information or assets.

It is understood and confirmed by this investigation that Wells Fargo Bank (Nevada National Bank) did not maintain retail branch banking operations inside Mexico during the relevant period, as Mexican banking law prohibited foreign branch deposit-taking institutions. Accordingly, this inquiry is directed specifically and exclusively to **Wells Fargo Express** — the freight, money-order, bullion-transport, and telegraphic wire-transfer arm of the company — whose documented presence in Mexico included roughly 223 offices and agents as of January 1900, expanding to 456 by January 1909, and continuing operations in Mexico until voluntary closure in 1964.

I note further that the 1905 corporate separation distinguished bank from express, that the 1910 Mexico restructuring produced Compañía Mexicana de Express, S.A. as a joint venture with Mexican postal authorities, and that the 1918 U.S. domestic nationalization of express companies did not affect the Mexican subsidiary, which continued as Wells Fargo Co. Express, S.A. through at least the 1960s.
