## Abstract (verbatim)

> This paper compares the consequences of different financial policies adopted in Mexico and Brazil in the decades before World War I. In the 1890s, the national governments of Mexico and Brazil pursued strikingly different policies toward banking regulation. In Brazil, after the fall of the monarchy, authorities briefly experimented with financial liberalization. In Mexico, in the same era, public officials created a banking system with more constraints and regulations. We compare the costs and benefits to the financial systems and the macroeconomic effects of these different banking regimes, thereby revisiting two classic concerns of financial historians, the costs of financial fragility versus the benefits of financial liberalization. We look at the outcomes for financial sectors and consider the differences in broad measures of overall economic performance under stress.

**Keywords:** financial regulation · financial crises · *encilhamento* · financial liberalization · financial repression.

## Why this matters for the TE360 case — what it anchors

### LEGAL pipe — Mexican banking regime 1890–1910

The paper documents the regulatory regime under which **all** Sonoran financial activity by Francisco Terminel and the Aviana-titled assets had to operate:

- **1884 Mexican Commercial Code** — granted Banamex broad monopoly privileges in exchange for an emergency loan to the government during the 1884 financial crisis. Set the legal frame for all Mexican private banking until the 1897 reform.
- **1888 Bleichroeder loan (Berlin)** — first foreign loan post-default, broke Banamex's monopoly hold on government credit, opened a two-year window for new provincial banks under Finance Minister Manuel Dublan.
- **May 1893 Limantour decree** — no new bank could open without treasury-minister approval and Code-of-Commerce conformance. *Closed the system until late 1890s.* Sonora provincial banking is shaped by this decree.
- **1897 Limantour banking law** — granted the first bank in each state tax-free status, penalized later banks with a 2% tax. Designed to accelerate financial deepening while limiting speculation. Authorized state banks (e.g. Banco de Nuevo León in four states) to continue cross-state operations.
- **1907 Panic** — the international recession tested Mexico's banking system; only Yucatán suffered serious problems. Finance Ministry response included founding an agricultural bank for longer-term credits, merger of weakest provincials, conversion of emission banks to investment banks (Gómez 2011). *This is the regulatory window in which Aviana's 1907 asserted deed would have been recorded — and in which the post-Panic agrarian-bank reforms reshaped Sonoran rural credit.*

### WF pipe — Mexico's banking ≠ Wells Fargo Express

The paper confirms the structural point the WF letter already makes: **Mexico's commercial banking system was domestic** (Banamex / Banco de Londres / state banks of emission). Wells Fargo Bank could not retail-bank inside Mexico. Wells Fargo Express was the express / money-order / bullion-transport channel, separate from the commercial banking system the paper describes. Anyone tracing "where Aviana's money went" needs to understand:

- **Banamex 1890 = 67% of bank-note circulation** → 1895 = 56% → 1900 = 36% → 1905 = 28%.
- **Banco de Londres** = ~31% (1890s), → 21% (1905).
- **State banks** = 6% (1890) → 13% (1895) → 35% (1900) → 51% (1905).

The shift toward state-bank emission during the exact window of Aviana's 1907 deed means the *state-level* Sonora banking record (not Banamex / Banco de Londres alone) is where the relevant 1905–1910 paper trail sits. **This sharpens the AHES inquiry priorities** — local Sonoran issue banks (Banco de Sonora, Banco Occidental de México, etc.) are now likelier search targets than the national-level institutions.
